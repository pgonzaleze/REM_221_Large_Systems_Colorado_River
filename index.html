The River That No Longer Reaches the Sea

The Colorado River supports more than 40 million people and more than two million hectares of farmland. Then, a short distance from the sea it once fed, it runs out. How did that happen, who pays, and what would you do next?

1 · The river

A plumbed river

Imagine a tiny stream in a wet meadow at La Poudre Pass, Colorado. Within a mile of its source it is already being redirected: the Grand Ditch sends water across the Continental Divide.

La Poudre Pass→Lee’s Ferry (Upper/Lower Basin divide)→Glen Canyon Dam · Lake Powell (1966)→Hoover Dam · Lake Mead→Parker and Imperial Dams→Morelos Dam · Mexico
  • Near Parker Dam the first big diversions leave: the aqueduct to Los Angeles and San Diego, and the Central Arizona Project to Phoenix and Tucson.
  • At Imperial Dam most of what remains goes to the All-American Canal, which can carry about the whole pre-dam natural flow and supplies winter vegetables for much of North America.
  • At the border, Morelos Dam diverts nearly all the rest to the Mexicali Valley.
  • Average flow falls from ≈14,180 cubic feet per second at Davis Dam to ≈2,060 at the Mexican border, about an 85% drop.
  • Since the 1960s the river has reached the delta by snowmelt only in 1983–86.

Why the river matters

The Colorado supplies drinking water for about 40 million people, irrigates roughly 5.5 million acres and is linked to about $1.4 trillion of economic activity a year (We Build Value, 2022). It rises at about 3,100 m in the Rockies and runs roughly 2,330 km through seven U.S. states. About 80% of its water goes to agriculture, and about 80% of that to forage crops such as alfalfa.

Reservoirs: the river’s savings account

  • Lake Mead, behind Hoover Dam, is the largest U.S. reservoir by capacity (about 28.9 million acre-feet when full). Lake Powell is the second.
  • In June 2022 the two lakes held only about 28% of their combined capacity (Bureau of Reclamation, quoted by We Build Value). Richter et al. put them about three-quarters empty by the end of 2022.
  • Dead pool: reported in 2022 as the level, about 895 feet at Lake Mead, below which water can no longer flow through Hoover Dam to California, Arizona and Mexico. Hydropower would stop earlier.
  • Las Vegas’s “third straw”: the city draws about 90% of its water from Lake Mead. Its first intake (1971) began to be exposed as the lake fell, so a tunnel under the lake bed (Intake 3, completed 2017) now draws from the bottom. A fix for one city does not add water to the river.

The pressure builds

  • In 2021 the federal government declared the first official shortage, cutting deliveries to Arizona, Nevada and Mexico.
  • Upstream, about 80% of Colorado’s river use is agriculture and 10% municipal and industrial. Trans-basin projects, such as the Colorado–Big Thompson Project, send river water under the mountains to Front Range farms and cities.

Two voices (2022 Senate hearings)

Southern Nevada Water Authority: the outlook is bleak but not unsolvable; the way out is demand management once thought unattainable, including careful crop choice and irrigation efficiency.
Family Farm Alliance: Western farming is a strategic resource for food security, rural communities and drought resilience, and cannot simply be replaced.

2 · The rules

A river divided on paper

In 1922, negotiators divided a river they believed was larger than it is. The delta was not at the table.

  • 1922 Compact: Upper and Lower Basins each get half. Mexico’s share is not specified.
  • 1944 Water Treaty: Mexico gets 1.5 million acre-feet a year, 22 years later.
  • 1963: the U.S. Supreme Court sets volumes for California, Arizona and Nevada.
  • 2001–2023: a run of cuts: California’s settlement (≈14%), the 2007 Interim Guidelines, Drought Contingency Plans, and Minutes 319 (2012), 323 (2017) and 330 (2024) with Mexico.
  • 2026: the 2007 guidelines and related agreements expire. The seven U.S. states fail to agree on a successor. Reclamation’s Final EIS (31 July 2026) sets a U.S. framework from 2027.

Many scientists argue the cuts so far still fall short of balancing use with supply.

How the rules fit together

  • The rules are a patchwork of a compact, a treaty, court decisions and federal guidelines written in different decades by different parties.
  • Upper and Lower Basin: Lee’s Ferry divides them, and each was allocated half the flow in 1922. California, Arizona and Nevada hold their volumes through the 1963 court decision.
  • Mexico: the Compact did not say how much Mexico should get; its share was set in 1944. Minutes are later addenda agreed by the U.S. and Mexican sections of the International Boundary and Water Commission (IBWC).
  • Shortage rules: since 2007 deliveries to Arizona and Nevada fall as Lake Mead drops to set levels. Minutes 319 and 323 let Mexico share shortages in a similar way.

Why the rules are hard to change

  • Water rights are legal property: cutting one user means negotiating with all of them, and each state fears giving up the most.
  • Today the table includes seven states, the federal government, 30 tribal nations and Mexico.
  • The agreements were written assuming more water than the river carries. Since 2000, consumption has exceeded supply in 16 of 21 years.
  • Cuts since 2000 have helped, but many scientists say they still fall short of balancing use with 21st-century supply.

2026: the deadline arrives

The 2007 guidelines and related agreements end in 2026. The Upper and Lower Basin states stayed deadlocked past repeated deadlines, so the federal government published its own Final Environmental Impact Statement (31 July 2026) to set a framework for U.S. operations from 2027. Water for Mexico is handled separately, through the IBWC and the 1944 Treaty.

3 · The delta

The sea loses its river

Picture 3,000 square miles of marsh where fresh water met Gulf tides, and a tidal bore the locals called “El Burro”. Today the delta is mostly desiccated.

  • Before: a nutrient-rich estuary and nursery for corvina, totoaba and shrimp.
  • Now: flow often ends below Morelos Dam; salinity is up; wetlands are estimated to have shrunk from ≈1.9 million to ≈150,000 acres.
  • The Cienega de Santa Clara survives largely on agricultural drainage water.
  • The system responds: floods in the 1980s and 1990s revived vegetation and boosted fish and shrimp catches. In 2014, Minute 319 allowed a deliberate pulse flow of 130 million cubic metres.
  • Not one cause: overfishing, gillnet bycatch and the illegal totoaba trade also hit the Upper Gulf. Scientists disagree about how much of the vaquita’s decline is due to flow.

A channel that stopped behaving like a river

  • The big changes began when Hoover Dam was completed in 1936. In the early 1940s sediment-starved spillway releases cut the river channel deeper in the first ≈50 km of the delta.
  • Today much of the channel is intermittent or dry. Flows returned in the 1980s and 1990s when the upstream reservoirs filled and spilled (Mueller et al., 2017).
  • Because the river’s water is fully allocated, rare big floods from the Gila River basin are the most likely cause of major physical change for the foreseeable future.

Plants, birds and salt

  • The riparian corridor is internationally important bird habitat. Its plants rely mostly on a shallow aquifer fed by underflow from irrigated fields in both countries; surface flows are needed to flush salts and germinate native trees (Hinojosa-Huerta et al., 2013).
  • In a 2002–2007 drought native cottonwoods and willows declined, introduced tamarisk shrubs spread, and bird species that need riparian habitat decreased while generalists increased. Total vegetation cover held near 70%, so parts of the system proved resilient.
  • The delta and Upper Gulf still hold about 37,890 ha of tidal and brackish wetlands used by large numbers of shorebirds, with tides of up to 5 m (Gomez-Sapiens et al., 2013).

The 2014 pulse flow: success or not?

From March to May 2014, Minute 319 allowed a pulse flow of 130 million m³ that travelled more than 100 km through the delta’s river corridor. Researchers read the result in two ways.

A proof of concept: it showed that environmental water could be delivered through irrigation infrastructure and that ecological responses could be monitored (Kendy et al., 2017).
Limited on its own: without active land management (clearing vegetation, grading land) new native trees established poorly and tamarisk benefited. Limits were seed supply, deep groundwater and competing plants. The low-magnitude flow wetted soils and lowered salinity but did not restore natural erosion and deposition (Shafroth et al.; Schlatter et al., 2017).

Lesson: water is necessary but not sufficient. Restoration needs water, land management, time and monitoring together.

4 · The people

Who lives at the end of the river

Think of a fishing town at the top of the Gulf, a farm in the Mexicali Valley, and a community whose name means “people of the river”.

  • Cucapá: fished and farmed in the delta for centuries. Estimates of ≈5,000 people long ago and ≈300 today are approximate. Flood releases from U.S. dams destroyed riverside homes and pushed the community to El Mayor.
  • El Golfo de Santa Clara: shrimp declined as fresh water fell; corvina became the main income.
  • Rules: the 1993 Biosphere Reserve, vaquita protections and a 2015 fishing ban restrict fishing. Alternatives are few, and ancestral rights are disputed.
  • Mexicali Valley: one of Mexico’s most fertile farming regions, fed by water diverted at Morelos Dam. In Mexico, irrigated crops are 86% of direct water use.

The social side of ecological loss

  • Culture: the Cucapá are tied to the river through farming, fishing and plants such as nipa saltgrass. Flood releases and a dry channel broke that link.
  • Voice: the 1922 Compact gave the delta no water. Delta communities now rely on binational work groups and NGOs rather than rights.
  • Conflict: conservation rules and Cucapá fishing claims have clashed with authorities and with other fishing communities.
A question of fairness: rules to protect the vaquita and the reserve respond to real ecological risk, but their costs fall on people who did not build the dams.

Stakeholder map (typical interests, a simplification)

  • Mexicali Valley irrigators: want reliable deliveries; fear cuts that threaten crops and jobs.
  • El Golfo fishers: want fresh water reaching the sea and a viable catch; fear bans without alternatives.
  • Cucapá communities: want recognised fishing rights and a living river; fear losing culture and place.
  • U.S. cities and Lower Basin farmers: want secure supply; fear deeper cuts and the cost of new infrastructure.
  • Upper Basin users: want to protect their allocation and rural economies.
  • Restoration groups and scientists: want water and money for the delta, which needs both countries.
  • Governments (IBWC, Reclamation, Mexican authorities): must hold a deal together with limited power over users.

5 · The numbers

An overdrawn account

Think of the reservoirs as a bank account. Inflow is income; use and evaporation are spending. The delta gets what is left.

Stocks and flows in plain words

  • Stock: what accumulates, here water stored in Lake Powell and Lake Mead.
  • Inflow: snowmelt and runoff. Warming reduces runoff, so the inflow is shrinking.
  • Outflow: consumptive use, evaporation, and what is left for the delta.
  • Overdraft: when outflow exceeds inflow the stock falls. It can go on for years because the stock hides the problem, which is a built-in delay.

More numbers (2000–2019 averages, Richter et al. 2024)

  • About 23.7 billion m³ (19.3 million acre-feet) consumed each year before the river reaches its delta.
  • Irrigated agriculture is 52% of all consumption and 74% of direct human use. Cattle-feed crops (alfalfa and hay) are 32% of all consumption and 62% of agricultural use, and about 90% of Upper Basin irrigation water.
  • About 12% leaves the basin through dozens of canals and pipelines; 19% is exported or used in Mexico, where irrigated crops are 80% of total consumption.
  • Riparian and wetland plants use 19%, including the Salton Sea and the Cienega de Santa Clara; reservoir evaporation 11%.
  • Policy has helped: Lower Basin use fell 38% for cities and industry and 15% for agriculture between 2000 and 2019.
  • Still needed: Schmidt and colleagues estimate cuts of 3–4 billion m³ a year (22–29% of direct use) to stabilise reservoirs, plus 1–3 billion m³ by 2050 if warming keeps reducing runoff.

Why alfalfa?

It tolerates variable climate, survives reduced irrigation in drought and recovers quickly (a “shock absorber”), fixes nitrogen, and is cheap to harvest by machine; demand is rising with the dairy industry. Plans to cut its water must compete with those advantages, through crop switching or paid fallowing.

Drought context

In June 2022 over 70% of the western U.S. was in severe or extreme drought and the basin was in its 23rd year of drought (Bureau of Reclamation, via We Build Value).

Decide · team workspace

It is Fall 2026

Lake Mead and Lake Powell are near record lows. The seven U.S. states have not agreed on rules beyond 2026. Under the 1944 Treaty Mexico is entitled to 1.5 million acre-feet a year, and since 2022 it has shared U.S. shortage cuts. The measures in Minutes 323 and 330 end on 31 December 2026. Your delegation must draft what comes next.

Your mission

Your team is a binational delegation drafting the next Minute under the 1944 Treaty. Each member plays one stakeholder and argues for it. The team must agree one draft that every role could sign. The numbered boxes below are the articles of your Minute.

How a Minute is built (your template)
Preamble (why this deal) → 1 Reductions → 2 Term → 3 Environment → 4 Storage and deferral → 5 Review and triggers → 6 Other provisions → 7 Signatures. Real Minutes are short documents agreed by the U.S. and Mexican sections of the IBWC. Yours should be short and specific too.

Step 1 · Choose roles (2 min)

One role per member (double up in small teams). Open your role card and note your goals, your red line and what you can offer. These are simplified interests, not quotes from real people.

U.S. section of the IBWC
  • Who you are: the U.S. commissioner who runs the treaty with Mexico and answers to Washington and the seven states.
  • You want: a deal that protects Lake Mead and Lake Powell, keeps relations with Mexico, and can be signed before 2026 ends.
  • Your red line: a deal that U.S. states or Congress would reject, or that looks generous to Mexico while Arizona and Nevada cut.
  • You can offer: shared shortage cuts, storage for Mexico in U.S. reservoirs, joint monitoring.
  • Opening position: Cut about the same as Lower Basin users (10–20%). Term 2–5 years. Delta money only if Mexico co-funds. Storage allowed if reservoirs benefit. Triggers tied to Lake Mead levels.
Mexican section of the IBWC
  • Who you are: the Mexican commissioner defending Mexico’s 1.5 million acre-foot treaty entitlement while seeking fairness.
  • You want: cuts no bigger than what U.S. users accept, the right to store and defer water, and recognition of the delta’s needs.
  • Your red line: larger cuts than U.S. users, or a domestic backlash from farmers.
  • You can offer: sharing shortages, cooperation on the environment and water quality.
  • Opening position: Cut 5–10%, matching what U.S. Lower Basin users give up. Short term (1–2 years) because of uncertainty. Delta a shared responsibility. Strong storage and deferral rights. Automatic review if shortages worsen.
Mexicali Valley irrigators
  • Who you are: farmers in one of Mexico’s most fertile regions, supplied by the water diverted at Morelos Dam. Irrigated crops are 86% of direct water use in Mexico.
  • You want: predictable deliveries, advance notice of cuts, support to use less (efficiency, paid fallowing) and a voice in decisions.
  • Your red line: a sudden deep cut that threatens crops, jobs and the valley’s economy.
  • You can offer: efficiency investments, paid fallowing, crop changes if supported.
  • Opening position: Cut under 10%. Short term. Delta water only if farmers are not the only ones paying. Storage and deferral yes. Farmers consulted before any deeper cut.
Delta communities and fishers
  • Who you are: fishing and Indigenous communities (El Golfo, the Cucapá) at the end of the river, with no water allocation of their own.
  • You want: water reaching the delta, restoration funding, recognised fishing rights, a seat at the table and alternative livelihoods.
  • Your red line: being left out again, or bans on fishing with nothing to replace the income.
  • You can offer: local knowledge, monitoring, restoration labour, and legitimacy for the deal.
  • Opening position: Any cut must leave something for the delta. Term long enough to restore (5+ years) with reviews. A guaranteed yearly flow and a dedicated fund. No storage rule that removes delta water. Ecological triggers too.
Lower Basin U.S. water users
  • Who you are: cities and farms in Arizona, California and Nevada that are already cutting (Las Vegas, Phoenix, Imperial Valley).
  • You want: fair sharing with Mexico cutting alongside, secure supply and a stable Lake Mead.
  • Your red line: carrying more than their share, or paying for others’ infrastructure.
  • You can offer: more conservation, funding for efficiency, support for storage.
  • Opening position: Cut 10–20% to match their own. Term tied to U.S. post-2026 rules (2–3 years). Delta support acceptable if small. Storage only if it does not harm reservoirs. Triggers tied to Lake Mead elevation.

Step 2 · Negotiate (10–12 min)

  1. Opening statements (1 min each): “I represent ___. We need ___. We can offer ___.”
  2. Take the articles in order, boxes 1 to 6 below. Start with the cut, because it shapes the rest.
  3. Trade, don’t just argue. Every concession should buy something, for example: “we accept a bigger cut if the deal includes storage flexibility and water for the delta.”
  4. Write treaty-style text: short, specific, with numbers and who does what.
  5. Sign or refuse (box 7). Each role says whether it would sign. A role that refuses or signs with reservations says why in box 8.

Step 3 · Write your draft Minute

How to answer
Write in full sentences, about 1–3 per box. Use the facts from your teammates’ tabs. Your reasoning matters more than matching what really happened.

Write each member’s name and the role they played (for example: Ana, Mexicali Valley irrigators).

Choose how much Mexico’s yearly water deliveries should fall, compared with its treaty allotment of 1.5 million acre-feet. Who cares most: Mexican section, Mexicali irrigators, Lower Basin users.

Short deals are flexible but must be renegotiated; long deals give certainty but lock in assumptions about a changing climate. Who cares most: U.S. section, delta communities.

Say whether the deal gives the delta any water (for example a yearly spring flow) or money (restoration, monitoring). Give a rough amount and who pays. Example: “A small yearly flow each spring, funded by both countries.” Who cares most: delta communities, both IBWC sections.

Can Mexico store water (for example in Lake Mead) or delay deliveries in wet or dry years? Write the rule in one or two sentences and say who benefits. Who cares most: Mexican section, Mexicali irrigators.

Name a condition that reopens the deal early, such as a reservoir level or a drought forecast, and what happens then. Example: “If Lake Mead is forecast below ___ feet, both countries meet to consider deeper cuts.” Who cares most: U.S. section, Lower Basin users.

Add one or two issues the first five boxes missed, such as water quality, shared data, compensation for farmers, or a seat at the table for fishing and Indigenous communities. Say what the deal does about each. Who cares most: everyone, especially delta communities and irrigators.

Would each role sign this draft? A real Minute needs both governments to sign, and every role has to live with it.

In two or three sentences: the main trade-off your team accepted, who gains or loses, and why any role refused or signed with reservations. Use the ideas from class (boundaries, feedbacks, delays) where they help.

Imagine it is the end of your "term" (length of the deal) and your deal has been in force. In 3–5 sentences describe (a) the reservoirs, (b) Mexicali farmers, (c) the delta, and one unintended consequence.
